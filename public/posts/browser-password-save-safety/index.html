Saving passwords in your browser: personal and shared-device rules

By KYD Editorial · First published 2026-07-14 · Last editorial review 2026-10-05

English edition adapted on 2026-10-05. The original source-check date is retained below; this is not a fresh product or match investigation.

Browser password storage can make unique, long passwords practical. Its suitability depends on the device, the operating-system account and the protection around both—not just convenience.

Use it on a device you control

Chrome, Edge and Safari can store credentials and fill them on later visits. On a personal, locked device, this can help avoid reusing one short password everywhere. It does not remove the need for two-step verification, current recovery information and attention to the login address.

EnvironmentPractical ruleAdditional check
Personal device with a screen lockConsider password storageUse unique passwords and two-step verification
Shared family computerUse separate operating-system accountsDo not store private credentials in a shared account
Library, hotel or other public computerDo not save credentialsSign out and check what remains after use

Review what is already saved

Open the browser’s password manager and inspect the site list. Remove obsolete credentials carefully, ensuring that you still have the access information you need. Review unfamiliar entries rather than assuming that a long list is harmless. Menu placement varies by browser version.

Protect the device itself

Use the device’s login password, PIN or supported biometric lock. On a phone without a screen lock or a computer left signed in, another person may have access to browser sessions and credentials. Locking the device is a separate layer from protecting each online account.

A saved password is not a signed-in session

A website may keep you signed in even when the browser did not save its password. Conversely, saving a password does not mean that you remain signed in after logging out. On a shared device, declining the save prompt is not enough: sign out of the website and the browser account as appropriate.

Choose one main storage workflow

Built-in managers are easy to start with and may synchronize through a browser account. A dedicated manager may suit people using several browsers or devices. Review the actual service’s security and recovery options. Whichever you choose, use different passwords for different sites, protect important accounts with two-step verification and avoid public-device storage.

If credentials were left on a public device

From a trusted device, change the affected password and review signed-in devices and account activity. Remove suspicious sessions and review recovery details. Delete the stored credentials from the old computer if you can, but do not rely on deletion alone: someone may already have used or copied them. Password changes and session controls have different effects depending on the service.
