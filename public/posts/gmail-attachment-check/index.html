Email attachments: seven checks before you open a file

By KYD Editorial · First published 2026-07-12 · Last editorial review 2026-10-05

English edition adapted on 2026-10-05. The original source-check date is retained below; this is not a fresh product or match investigation.

An invoice, meeting document or school notice can look routine while carrying an unexpected file or a phishing link. Check the context and requested action as well as the displayed sender name.

Seven checks, in order

  1. Actual sender address: expand the sender details and inspect the complete domain. A display name is not proof of identity.
  2. Expected context: were you waiting for this file, and does the wording match the conversation? A familiar account can itself be compromised.
  3. Complete extension: show extensions in Windows. Unexpected .exe, .js, .bat or .scr files are not ordinary documents.
  4. Archive contents: inspect filenames before running anything. Nested or password-protected archives deserve additional verification.
  5. Login destination: if a document redirects to a login screen, verify its address independently before entering credentials.
  6. Urgency and payment demands: an account suspension or immediate invoice deadline is a reason to pause, not skip checks.
  7. Independent confirmation: use a known phone number, established messenger conversation or official contact channel—not just a reply to the suspicious message.

Why ordinary-looking messages deserve care

Attackers can use familiar labels such as quotation, payroll, receipt, shipping notice or meeting material. Evaluate the sender, format, tone and action together. A file’s friendly name does not establish what it does when opened.

What to do if uncertain

Leave the file unopened and contact the expected sender through a channel you already know. Open any required service through its official app or a verified address. Chrome and Gmail warnings are additional signals; disabling them does not establish authenticity.

If you already opened it

Record whether you merely viewed a file, enabled a feature, installed software or entered credentials. Check for unwanted apps, browser changes and unfamiliar account activity. Protect affected accounts from a trusted device and review two-step verification. For a managed device, report to the responsible team rather than attempting an unverified cleanup yourself.

Keep organizational rules simple

Agree on a small set of memorable rules: do not run unexpected executables from email, independently verify password-protected archives, and revisit login requests through known official addresses. Make the reporting contact clear so that a suspicious attachment is reported without being forwarded broadly or opened for demonstration.
