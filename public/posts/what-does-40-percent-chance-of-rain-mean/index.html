What does a 40% chance of rain mean? Read the forecast before making plans

By KYD Editorial · First published 2026-10-06 · Last editorial review 2026-10-06

A 40% chance of rain describes a chance of measurable precipitation at the forecast location during a stated period. It does not tell you that rain will last for 40% of your day. Before changing your plans, check which place, which hours and which rainfall threshold the number describes.

The answer in one minute

Read the percentage as a probability, then look for its boundaries. “40% between 2pm and 3pm at the park” answers a different question from “40% tomorrow across a city forecast.” Neither statement tells you how many minutes you will get wet or how much water will fall.

The US National Weather Service explains precipitation probability in terms of a location and time period. Its example uses at least 0.01 inch, about 0.254 mm, of rain. The percentage alone is not a prediction that the same fraction of your town will get wet.

Three numbers that answer different questions
Forecast fieldWhat to askWhat it cannot answer alone
Chance of rain, such as 40%How likely is measurable rain here in this period?How heavy or how long?
Rainfall amount, such as 2 mmHow much water is forecast over the stated interval?At exactly what minute will it fall?
Hourly timing or weather symbolWhich part of my outing overlaps the forecast weather?Will my particular street definitely stay dry?

These sample numbers are separate illustrations, not a real forecast. Do not combine them into a prediction for your location.

Why the provider and time window matter

The Met Office forecast guide defines its hourly precipitation chance using at least 0.1 mm within one hour. Its three-hour forecast uses at least 0.3 mm within three hours. Those thresholds differ from the NWS example above. A percentage can look identical on two screens while referring to different events.

On Met Office tables, the precipitation probability, weather symbol and gust value represent the interval starting at the displayed time, rather than just that instant. Read your own provider’s explanation before assuming every app labels periods this way.

For a useful comparison, write down the location, date, interval, units and latest update from each forecast. If one is for your home and the other for a hilltop destination, settle that difference first. If one gives a full-day summary and the other an afternoon hour, open comparable intervals. A disagreement may become understandable before you need to decide which service to follow.

A worked example: a picnic with two possible start times

Imagine you are arranging a two-hour picnic. You can arrive at 10am or 1pm, and an indoor café is available nearby. The following invented forecast is a reading exercise, not a prediction or a tested recommendation.

Fictional forecast at one location, with hourly periods
PeriodRain chancePlanning question
10–11am20%Have we included the walk to the park?
11am–noon30%Can we pack up without a long exposed walk?
1–2pm60%Would we be happy to move indoors?
2–3pm70%Is the café available for the whole group?

For this imaginary choice, the morning has lower displayed hourly probabilities. That is a reason to investigate the morning slot; it is not proof that the picnic will stay dry. You still need rainfall intensity, wind, temperature and any warnings. If the café is unavailable, the cost of getting the choice wrong changes even though the forecast does not.

Also include setup and the trip home. A picnic scheduled to finish at noon may still leave you outside at 12:30. A plan based only on the advertised start time misses the part of the journey where you have fewer options.

Do not add hourly percentages

Two hours showing 40% do not automatically make an 80% chance of rain during the outing. Adding the numbers counts overlapping possibilities twice. Nor can you simply average them to obtain a correct two-hour probability.

As a mathematical illustration, if two events each had a 40% chance and were independent, the chance of at least one would be 1 − (0.6 × 0.6) = 64%. If rain in the two hours always occurred together, the combined chance would instead be 40%. Both examples use the same hourly percentages but different relationships between the hours. Actual weather can persist or move between periods, so the independence assumption is not supplied by the display. Use a provider’s forecast for the whole interval where available; do not turn this calculation into a homemade weather forecast.

Match the decision to the consequence

There is no universal percentage at which everyone should cancel. Consider what you need to protect, how long you will be exposed and how quickly you can change plans. These are KYD planning questions, not official warning thresholds.

  • A short errand: decide whether carrying a raincoat is easier than returning home for one.
  • A meal outdoors: reserve a realistic indoor alternative and agree who will tell the group about a change.
  • A photo session: protect equipment and identify a covered location before leaving; weather damage is a different concern from personal discomfort.
  • A hike or exposed event: consult local warnings and route-specific advice. A low rain percentage does not certify a route as safe.

A backup plan is most useful when it includes a decision time. “We will check again before leaving and meet indoors if the organizer changes the venue” is clearer than “We will see how it goes.” Choose that time around travel and cancellation constraints rather than inventing a supposedly scientific rain cutoff.

Thunderstorms need a separate decision

Rain probability is not a lightning safety rating. The NWS lightning guidance says to enter a substantial building or an enclosed metal-topped vehicle when thunder is heard, and remain sheltered for at least 30 minutes after the last thunder. An umbrella or open picnic shelter is not a substitute.

Check your local official alerts before an outdoor activity and follow instructions as conditions change. This article’s picnic example assumes you are deciding about ordinary rain; it cannot override a warning or on-site safety decision.

A forecast note you can reuse

Location and date: [place, local date].
Time outside: [travel, activity and return].
Forecast provider and update: [source, time].
Rain probability and interval: [percentage, start/end].
Amount, wind and warnings: [checked details].
Backup location: [confirmed option].
Next decision: [who checks, and when].

Common questions

Does 40% mean rain for 24 minutes in an hour?

No. That would confuse probability with duration. Look for separate information about the timing and character of the rain.

Should I choose the app with the lowest number?

First compare the same place and interval. Choosing the most convenient number does not resolve a mismatch in what the forecasts describe.

Can I use this page to check tomorrow’s weather?

Use a current local forecast for that. This guide teaches you how to read it; the example percentages above never update.
